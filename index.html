Innovative Learning

Game Design

Bridging the science of measurement with the art of play — designing games that reveal learning and make assessment meaningful.

Featured Project

Poemancer

Poemancer is a card game that blends poetry, wordplay, and strategic thinking into a unique tabletop experience. Players craft verses, cast words like spells, and compete in poetic duels — making language itself the core mechanic of play.

The game sits at the intersection of literary creativity and game design, creating an engaging context for players to explore the expressive power of language. It reflects a broader vision: that play can be a meaningful vehicle for developing literacy, creativity, and aesthetic sensibility.

Design Philosophy

Why Games?

Games create contexts where engagement is intrinsic, failure is safe, and learning happens naturally through play. As a researcher in educational measurement, I believe well-designed games can serve as powerful assessment instruments — revealing what people know and can do in ways that traditional tests often cannot. Poemancer is an exploration of that idea applied to language and creativity.

Related Academic Work

Games in Education & Measurement

At the University of Massachusetts Amherst, I designed and taught a First Year Seminar on Principles of Game Design Applied in Education (Fall 2021), exploring how game mechanics can enhance learning experiences. I have also presented work on game-based assessment at NCME, including a demonstration of Reliability Challenge! — a game designed to teach classical test theory concepts in an engaging, hands-on format.

Community Building

Creating spaces where measurement professionals connect, advocate, and grow — from founding a Chilean professional forum to leading graduate student initiatives at the national level in the US.

Co-founder & Leader · Since 2020

FEVED — Foro Chileno de Profesionales en Evaluación Educacional

In 2020, Sergio co-founded FEVED, the Chilean Forum of Professionals in Educational Assessment — a multidisciplinary advocacy group dedicated to improving educational measurement practice in Chile. The group brings together researchers, psychometricians, and policy professionals committed to evidence-based, equitable testing standards.

FEVED has organized national seminars, published open letters on high-stakes assessments such as the PAES and SIMCE, produced a webinar series on validity with Spanish-speaking experts, and actively participated in public debates on education policy in Chile.

Sergio served as its founding Coordinator (2020–2021) and has continued his involvement as Vice-Coordinator (March 2025 – February 2026), working alongside Loretta Labarca.

Chair · July 2021 – May 2023

NCME Graduate Student Issues Committee (GSIC)

Sergio served as Chair of the NCME Graduate Student Issues Committee (GSIC), the body responsible for representing the voice of graduate students within the National Council on Measurement in Education. During his term he organized social events, coordinated sessions at the annual conference, and served as liaison between students and the NCME board. His tenure as Senior Chair at the 2023 Annual Meeting in Chicago received strong recognition from the board.

Coordinator · October 2021 – April 2023

NCME-GSIC Standards Study Group

Sergio led the NCME-GSIC Standards Study Group, a graduate student initiative created to engage the next generation of measurement professionals in critically reviewing the Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing. The group ran a two-week cycle per chapter — one week of discussion among students, followed by a Q&A session with an expert on that topic.

The study group culminated in a co-authored statement on behalf of graduate students with recommendations for the new edition of the Standards, published as a white paper and presented at NCME 2023.

Psychometric Consultancy for Randomized Tests

Expert guidance on designing, analysing, and validating assessments that use randomly parallel forms — balancing test security, reliability, and fairness.

What it is

Randomly parallel tests — assessments where each examinee receives a unique form drawn from a common item pool — offer powerful protections against pre-knowledge and cheating. But they introduce specific psychometric challenges around reliability, equating, and score comparability. This service provides end-to-end support for organisations implementing this testing model, grounded in published research on the topic.

What's included
🏗️

Test Blueprint & Item Pool Design

Guidance on designing item pools large and diverse enough to support randomly parallel administration, including content specifications and item exposure controls.

📊

Reliability Analysis for Parallel Forms

Estimation and interpretation of reliability under random parallel form conditions, including practical guidance on acceptable reliability thresholds.

🔒

Test Security Benefit Evaluation

Quantitative assessment of the security gains achieved through randomisation, including Score-Difference-at-Risk (SDaR) analysis to anticipate exposure impacts.

🔄

Equating & Score Comparability

Support for selecting and implementing appropriate equating methods to ensure scores are comparable across randomly generated forms.

Who is this for

Testing organisations, credentialing bodies, and universities exploring or already using randomly parallel test delivery who need rigorous psychometric support to do it right.

Cost Reduction

AI, done right, can dramatically reduce the cost of building and running assessments. I help organisations identify where it genuinely helps — and implement it with the methodological rigour that makes the savings real and sustainable.

The opportunity

Test development is expensive. Writing items, piloting them, reviewing them, scoring constructed responses, and generating equating data all consume significant resources. Generative AI and modern data methods can reduce the cost of each of these steps — but only if implemented carefully. Applied carelessly, AI creates new problems that cost more to fix than the original process. My published research sits at exactly this intersection: what AI can do, what it can't, and how to tell the difference.

Three ways AI reduces cost in assessment
✍️

AI-Assisted Item Construction

Writing items from scratch is the single largest cost in test development. LLMs like ChatGPT can generate draft items at scale — but quality, content fit, and psychometric properties vary widely depending on how the prompts are designed. My research has directly studied these questions: how different prompt engineering strategies perform across content areas, how humans, NLP models, and LLMs compare in generating binary response items, and how to build semi-automatic item generation workflows that produce usable content without sacrificing quality.

Related work: Prompt Engineering for Binary Response Item Generation using ChatGPT · Comparing Humans, NLP models and LLMs (ATP 2024) · Foster, C., Weaver, S., & Araneda, S. (2025). Using ChatGPT for Semi-Automatic Generation of Items for Summative Assessment

🧬

Synthetic & Augmented Data

Equating, calibration, and validation studies all require data — and collecting that data is costly. Synthetic data generated by LLMs acting as simulated respondents offers a promising way to reduce pilot testing costs and support psychometric analyses when real data is scarce or expensive to obtain. I have worked on using LLM-generated synthetic respondent data in combination with diagnostic classification models (DCMs) for equating, and on assessing the pre-exposure risks that arise when item pools are generated by AI — a risk dimension that didn't exist before LLMs.

Related work: Araneda, S., Lee, W.-C., & Kim, S. (2025). Equating forms using synthetic data via LLM respondents and DCM models · Foster, C., & Araneda, S. (2026). Assessing Pre-Exposure Risks of LLM-Generated Item Pools

📝

Automatic & AI-Assisted Scoring

Scoring constructed-response items — essays, short answers, performance tasks — is among the most labour-intensive parts of assessment. Automatic scoring systems powered by AI can drastically reduce that cost, but they require careful validation to ensure the scores they produce are interpretable and fair. My work at ETS AI Labs and College Board included direct engagement with automatic essay scoring for the SAT, and my research provides frameworks for validating AI tools used in testing that are grounded in measurement theory rather than just technical benchmarks.

Related work: Securing Tests with AI: Initial Explorations in Item Generation (NCME SIGIMIE 2024) · Araneda, S. (2025). Function ascription, use-plans and how to validate AI tools used in educational testing

Who this is for

Organisations that want to use AI to reduce assessment costs but need someone who understands both what the technology can do and what the psychometric requirements are — so the savings don't come with hidden validity risks attached.

The socio-technical perspective situates your test within its broader social context, identifying the structural and design choices that create risk before they become headlines. Together, they produce a validity and fairness record that is both technically rigorous and socially accountable.

Growth

Most assessment programs focus entirely on what their tests measure. The ones that grow — that build loyalty, reduce attrition, and earn the trust of the people they serve — also pay attention to how it feels to take them.

The opportunity

Test-taker experience is the most underinvested dimension of assessment design. Organisations spend enormous resources on item quality, reliability, and security — and almost nothing on what it actually feels like to sit in front of their test. That neglect has real costs: candidates who disengage mid-test, examinees who leave with negative impressions of the certifying body, programs that struggle with attrition and re-registration rates, and tests that fail to elicit the genuine cognitive engagement that valid score interpretation requires.

The organisations that grow are the ones that make their test-takers feel something other than dread. I can help you get there — by designing experience requirements into your test from the ground up, auditing what your current experience produces, and bringing tools from game design to create assessments that are genuinely engaging without sacrificing an ounce of rigor.

Experiential Approach

A framework for treating the test-taker's lived experience as a legitimate and necessary source of validity evidence — developed through doctoral research and extended through ongoing work in measurement theory and assessment design.

What this area of expertise is

The experiential approach is a framework I developed in my dissertation for understanding and studying what test-takers go through during an assessment — cognitively, emotionally, and socially — and using that understanding to inform test design and validation. It draws on phenomenology, socio-cognitive validity theory, and qualitative and mixed-methods research traditions to produce evidence that standard psychometric analyses cannot generate. The core argument is that experience is not incidental to what a test does: it is constitutive of it. How a test feels shapes how examinees engage, what their scores mean, and what the assessment does to them as people. A validity framework that ignores this is incomplete.

This work connects naturally to fairness, human-centred design, and the growing demand for assessments that are not just technically sound but socially accountable. It is the methodological foundation for what I offer under both the Growth and Insurance consulting areas.

Tests as Socio-Technical Artifacts

A theoretical framework for understanding assessments not as neutral measurement instruments but as designed objects — shaped by human decisions, embedded in social systems, and productive of real-world consequences that extend far beyond the score report.

What this area of expertise is

The socio-technical artifacts framework applies ideas from the philosophy of technology — particularly theories of artifact function, use-plans, and the social embedding of technical objects — to the domain of educational testing. A test is not simply a measurement device. It is a designed artifact that encodes choices about what counts as knowledge, who gets measured and how, what counts as a valid response, and what decisions scores are permitted to support. These choices are never neutral: they reflect the values, constraints, and interests of the people and institutions that build and use the test.

This perspective has direct practical implications. It surfaces sources of construct-irrelevant variance that purely psychometric analysis misses, identifies validity threats that emerge from institutional context rather than item quality, and provides a language for discussing the social consequences of assessment that goes beyond conventional consequential validity. It also shapes how I think about validating AI tools in assessment — tools that are themselves socio-technical artifacts with their own embedded assumptions and use-plans.

Randomly Parallel Testing

A specialised area of psychometric research focused on assessments where every examinee receives a unique, randomly assembled form drawn from a common item pool — balancing test security, reliability, and fairness in ways that standard test design frameworks were not built to handle.

What this area of expertise is

Randomly parallel tests assign each examinee a unique form constructed on the fly from a shared item pool, rather than a fixed form shared across all examinees. This model offers powerful protection against pre-knowledge and item sharing — common threats in high-stakes testing — but it introduces psychometric challenges that are distinct from those of conventional fixed-form or adaptive tests. Reliability is affected by form-to-form variability; the security benefit depends critically on pool size and item exposure rates; score comparability requires careful attention; and the relationship between randomisation and fairness is more complex than it first appears.

My published and ongoing research addresses these challenges directly — developing frameworks for measuring the test security benefit of randomisation, analysing reliability under random parallel conditions, and assessing the risks that arise when AI is used to generate the item pools that feed these tests. This is a niche where deep specialist knowledge makes a concrete difference to how programs are designed and evaluated.

My work on randomly parallel testing is conducted in my capacity as a Research Scientist at Caveon, the leading test security company in the industry and the originator of many of the methods and technologies in this space. Thought Leadership

A Vision for the Future

Reflections on where educational measurement is heading — and what it would take for the field to be both rigorous and sustainable.

Core Argument

Toward a Sustainable Technique of Educational Measurement

My starting point is a simple but demanding claim: the goal of educational measurement should not be to preserve its current practices, but to make itself sustainable over time. A sustainable technique of testing is one that can coexist in a world where its use does not cause harm — coherent, in other words, with a project of social justice.

This is not a vague aspiration. It has concrete implications for what we treat as foundational. It means that ethics cannot be an afterthought — our field's codes of professional responsibility, the legal frameworks within which we operate, the rights of the people whose lives are shaped by test scores, these are not peripheral considerations. They are what makes the technique defensible. Without them, we are left with technical competence in the service of goals we have never examined.

It also means we need to be more honest about the politics of what we do. Educational measurement has never been a neutral enterprise. We take decisions about people's lives — sometimes about their very futures. The question is not whether our profession is political, but whether we will acknowledge that, and build our practice accordingly. A field that locates itself in an ivory tower, insulated from values and from the communities it affects, is not just philosophically naïve. It is professionally fragile.

This argument is developed at length in a commentary I co-authored with John Fremer and David Foster, published in Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice (2024), in direct response to the task force on foundational competencies in our field. The paper argues that the consensus document the field produced was not disruptive enough — that it describes what practitioners currently do rather than imagining what they need to become. What is missing is precisely this: a vision of testing as a technique that can sustain itself by earning the trust of the people it serves.

Early-Career Scholar Interview · NCME, April 2025

On Paths, Community, and What the Field Still Needs to Become

In April 2025, NCME interviewed me as part of a series on early-career scholars. The conversation covers my non-linear path into educational measurement — from applied mathematics in Chile, through several years working in finance technology, to eventually finding my way into the field through a growing sense that I wanted my work to be meaningful, and that education was where that meaning was.

A central theme in the interview is what my engineering background gave me: not just technical tools, but a particular way of seeing testing. For me, a test is a form of technology — something both technical and societal at once. It has strict, quantifiable properties, but also implications that ripple across many levels of people's lives. I describe it as engineering a system, perhaps the most serious kind of game we build as a society. That framing — testing as a socio-technical object — emerges organically from the way I was trained to think, rather than as an abstract theoretical stance.

The interview also touches on what I find most rewarding about the field: its demand for the full breadth of a person — mathematical, political, emotional. And on what I most want to see change. The thing I feel most urgently about is rehumanizing our profession. Our field carries a history of dehumanization — of reducing people to competitive characteristics, of modeling from nowhere rather than from the bodies and places we actually inhabit. I think we can do better. I think we can build a technique of testing that breaks that pattern. That is what I would most like to see happen.

I also speak about the community-building work I did through NCME's Graduate Student Issues Committee and the founding of FEVED in Chile — experiences I did not anticipate and that turned out to matter more than almost anything else I did during my graduate years.
